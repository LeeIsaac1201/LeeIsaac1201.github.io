PawsBase

A solo UX redesign of the health log timeline and entry flow in PawsBase, a Flutter pet health & training application I develop on as part of a group project. Within that project, I was also credited with designing the Add New Pet page; the health log redesign below is a separate exploration I took on myself, distinct from my assigned role.

role: developer on team credited design: Add New Pet page self-directed: health log redesign tools: Figma, Flutter
// 01 — overview

Project overview

Goals

Improve the health log timeline and entry flow, the part of PawsBase where a pet owner reviews their pet's history and logs new records (vaccinations, checkups, medication). The application works; the goal here is to make this specific flow clearer and faster to use, based on real observation rather than assumption.

Target audience & context

Pet owners tracking ongoing health and training records for their pets. I am a developer on the PawsBase team (I built the original version of these two screens), and this case study is my own solo redesign exploration, separate from what the team has shipped, applying a proper UX process to a flow I already know well from the inside.

// 02 — process

Design process

01

Research

Interviewed 3 pet owners, anchored around friction points found in the current code (one form for all entry types, no weight history, tight modal). Found a clear split: two wanted structured, type-specific detail and weight trends over time; one wanted a single quick note and nothing more. All three valued vaccination reminders; medication/checkup reminders were more polarizing.

02

Ideation

The clearest signal was a tension between minimal and thorough users, resolved almost exactly as one participant suggested it themselves: a quick-capture default for fast logging, with an optional expand into type-specific fields (vaccine name/next due, dosage/frequency, vet notes) plus a weight entry for trend tracking.

03

Wireframing

No separate wireframing stage. Without edit access to the team's Figma file, I went directly from the research finding to building the real screen in Flutter, using the progressive disclosure decision as the direct blueprint rather than sketching it first.

04

Prototyping

Built as a real, working Flutter screen using the application's actual design tokens and data structure, rather than a Figma click-through. Testing a working screen meant people's reactions were to a real interaction, not a simulation of one.

05

Testing

Tested the working prototype with 3 people. One filled in only the visible fields and saved without noticing "Add more detail" existed at all. One paused, explored, and found it naturally when asked to add a next-due date. One deliberately ignored it and, when shown the expanded state, said it looked "busy" even while understanding it was optional. Progressive disclosure worked for two of three, but missed a real discoverability gap for someone who does not proactively explore the screen.

// 03 — artefacts

Design artefacts

Credited design work on the team

Separate from the health log redesign above, I was the credited designer for the Add New Pet and Edit Pet pages within the live PawsBase application. This is shipped, team-approved work, not part of the self-directed exploration.

// 04 — decisions

Key design decision & trade-off

"A quick pop-up for fast notes, but with the option to expand into a full screen if I want to add more detail." One participant's own suggestion, and the decision that shaped the redesign.

The current form treats every entry type identically: same three fields, same modal, regardless of whether you are logging a five-minute checkup note or a full vaccination record. Interviews showed this one-size-fits-all approach does not actually fit anyone well. It is too sparse for owners who want vaccine names, dosages, and weight trends, and still too much friction for owners who just want to jot something down fast.

Rather than pick a side, the redesign follows progressive disclosure: a quick-capture default (type, one-line note, date) stays the fast path for anyone who wants it, with an "add more detail" expansion that reveals type-specific fields (vaccine name and next-due date, dosage and frequency, or vet notes) plus an optional weight entry. This was a genuine trade-off to accept. It is more implementation complexity than a single fixed form, and the interface has to clearly signal that more detail is optional, not required, or it risks feeling cluttered for the minimalist users it is also meant to serve.

// 05 — challenges & results

Challenges and outcomes

Challenges

Flutter and Dart were new to me going into this project, so on top of the design work, I was learning the language itself while trying to implement something reasonably polished. That made building a real Flutter screen instead of a Figma click-through slower than it should have been (wiring the type-specific fields, the expand/collapse state, and a standalone test harness so I could try it without touching the team's shipped application took longer while I was still learning the basics). It also meant testing gave more honest feedback than a prototype might have, since people were interacting with a real, working interface.

Results

The redesign resolved the core tension for most users. Someone who wanted to explore found "Add more detail" naturally, and someone who wanted a fast path could ignore it entirely. But testing surfaced a real gap: one participant missed it completely, which means the toggle is not discoverable enough on its own. With more time, the next iteration would make "Add more detail" a more visually distinct affordance (a filled button rather than a plain text link, for instance) rather than relying on people to notice it unprompted.
